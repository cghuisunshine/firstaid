3. Emergency Scene Management — Scene Survey & Consent

Instructor: Emergency scene management is this whiteboard over here. With Emergency Scene Management, it is your step-by-step guide. Emergency Scene Management is your step-by-step guide that is going to help you go about a first aid situation.

Instructor: So someone’s hurt, and they go, “Help! We need first aid!” And you arrive and you go, “What do I do first? I took the course, but I forgot what’s the first thing to do, and I’m nervous. I don’t... I don’t... I’m freezing.” Emergency Scene Management, ESM, is a step-by-step guide that’s going to help you feel more confident, and it’s going to make you feel more organized.

Instructor: The first step in Emergency Scene Management is Scene Survey. That’s the first step. And this is asking our detective questions, asking our nosy questions to get more information about the scene.

Instructor: What happened? What happened here? What happened before I got here? What is the history?

Instructor: Is it safe? Is the area safe? If it is, great. If the area is not safe, what do we do?

Student: Leave.

Instructor: Yeah. We can’t do anything. If the area is not safe, I’m not going to be a hero and run in. No. I’m going to injure myself. I’m going to be the next casualty. So we have to wait and make sure it’s safe. If we can make it safe in a safe way, great. But if it’s not safe, then just stand by. Maybe call 911. Our safety, by the way, comes first. Our safety is before the casualty, because we cannot help anybody if we are in danger, if we are injured.

Instructor: How many? How many people are injured? How many casualties are there? Because I’m just one first aider. Is it just one casualty? Is it just one injured person? If so, nice. I like that ratio: one to one. That’s good.

Instructor: If there’s two, three, four people injured here, now it’s a little bit more stressful. Now I have to do an extra step of triage. Now I have to prioritize: who do I help first? Who do I help second? Who do I help third?

Instructor: Last question: consent. “Hey, my name is Marwa. I’m a first aider. Can I help you?” “Yes, please help me!” Or, “No. Don’t help me. Don’t touch me. I don’t want your help.” What do we do?

Student: Leave it.

Instructor: Yeah. Don’t help. Someone said something.

Student: Call 911.

Instructor: Good. Someone is bleeding, they’re losing a lot of blood from their leg, and they say, “Nope, I don’t want help. Don’t touch me.” Okay? No means no. Yes means yes. We have to listen. But we can still call 911 and say, “Hey 911, this person is losing a lot of blood. They told me not to touch them. They said they don’t want help, but you should still come here.”

Instructor: So remember, with consent: yes means yes. No means no.

🔴 DEFINITELY ON EXAM: Implied consent.

Instructor: Implied consent is going to be on the exam. You need to know that when your casualty goes unresponsive, unalert, unconscious, you now have implied consent to help, which means yes. Even if they said no before.

Instructor: Let’s say they were like, “No, don’t touch me!” and they go unresponsive because they maybe lost a lot of blood. Now you have to help. Now it’s a yes. Even before they said no, now it’s a yes. Implied consent.

Instructor: How do I tell if I have implied consent? How do I tell if my casualty is alert or not? So AVPU is going to be an acronym that’s going to help us identify whether or not we have implied consent.

Students: Implied consent.

4. Primary Survey — ABCs + Rapid Body Survey

Instructor: For Primary Survey, up here, we asked all the information, all the questions we needed to know about the situation. We know it’s safe, we know it’s one person, we know what happened, we know we have our consent, whether it’s implied or a verbal yes.

Instructor: Now Primary Survey is going to give us information about the person, about their health. So Primary Survey includes the ABCs plus the RBS.

Instructor: ABCs stand for airway, breathing, circulation.

Airway

Instructor: When we check the airway, I’m going to do a head-tilt/chin-lift. One hand is going to go on the forehead, other hand is going to go underneath the chin, and you’re going to just lift up. When you lift up, it’s creating a clear airway.

Instructor: You’re going to open up their mouth to see if there’s anything inside the airway. Is there anything? Is their tongue lodged to the back of their throat? Is their throat swollen shut? What is preventing them from breathing?

Breathing

Instructor: Next, I’m going to check for breathing. I’m seeing for the chest to rise. Maybe their belly’s going to rise. That shows they’re breathing. I’m getting close to hear the breath and maybe feel it on my cheek.

Instructor: One-one-thousand, two-one-thousand, three-one-thousand, four-one-thousand, five-one-thousand. I’m counting breathing 5 to 10 seconds. If there’s no breathing after 5 seconds, count another 5. I want to make sure that they’re really not breathing.

Circulation

Instructor: After breathing is circulation. You’re checking the skin condition. Is their skin pale? Is it cold? Is it cool? Is it clammy? Clammy is just that sweaty feeling.

Instructor: Another way you can check circulation is by pressing down on the fingertips. If you press down on your nail, you’ll see that it goes yellow for like half a second, and then it goes back to that pinky color. If you press down on your casualty’s nail and the yellow stays longer, they have poor circulation.

Instructor: If they’re wearing nail polish or fake nails, you can just check the inside of their mouth. If someone has a darker complexion and it’s harder to tell if they’re pale or not, the inside of the mouth is useful. If the inside of the lips or the gums are blue or gray, not a good sign. They have poor circulation.

Rapid Body Survey

Instructor: After you check A, B, C, airway, breathing, circulation, we’re going to do something called an RBS. RBS stands for Rapid Body Survey. A Rapid Body Survey is what we are going to do to check for any injuries on this person.

Instructor: With the RBS, I want you to communicate out loud. Talk to your casualty, and if they’re not responsive, still communicate. Say what you’re doing.

Instructor: “Hey, I’m going to do a Rapid Body Survey just to check for any injuries on your person, okay? Starting with your head, neck. I’m going to check your breastbone, see if it’s sunken in. I’m going to check your arms down to your hands, other arm down to your hands. I’m going to check your back, waist, checking your legs down to your feet, other leg down to your feet.”

Instructor: Why is it important to talk? What does it look like I’m doing when I’m not talking? I’m robbing him. I’m looking for cash money and I’m leaving, right? So that’s why it’s so important to talk out loud. It’s an optics thing.

5. Transport Decision, Secondary Survey & Ongoing Care

Instructor: Transport Decision is now when we get to decide what to do. What do we do with this person? So the Transport Decision has three options: return to work, medical aid, and rapid transport.

Instructor: Rapid transport is when we call the ambulance. Medical aid is when we have an injury that’s maybe not too severe to call the ambulance, but we should still probably send them to the hospital or clinic. Return to work is when we take care of the injury and we can send them back.

Instructor: Rapid Transport, we either call 911 by ourselves, or we get someone to do it for us. “You in the green jacket, can you call 911 and let them know we have an unresponsive casualty? We’re at 604 Columbia Street. Activate the ERP, Emergency Response Plan, get me a first aid kit and an AED kit. Do you understand?” Make sure they understand.

Instructor: If your casualty has a decreased level of consciousness, if they’re unconscious, unalert, unresponsive, you would need to call 911. If their airway is not effective, their breathing’s not effective, their circulation’s not effective, you would have to call 911. If you do the Rapid Body Survey and you find many injuries and you can’t deal with it by yourself, call 911.

Instructor: Medical aid is something in the middle. You arrange transport yourself. So you call a taxi, an Uber, or maybe your workplace has a designated driver to send the casualty to the hospital or clinic. Arrange transport, stabilize the injury, and document.

Instructor: If they refuse to go to medical aid, you can’t force somebody to go. Just document it: “Person refused to leave against first aider’s advice.” Documentation is really good for you. It covers you.

Instructor: Return to Work is the third and final option. Treat the injury, advise the worker how to take care of the injury, do follow-up care, document, and you can recommend alternative duties. That’s your job as a first aider — alternative duties. Recommend them and talk to your supervisor.

Instructor: Secondary Survey is now where we can go, “I called 911 or help, whatever. Now let’s look closely at the injury. Let’s examine it.” Let’s check circulation and nerve function.

Instructor: Ongoing Care is just an extension of Secondary Survey. Ongoing Care is still when you’re waiting for ambulance to arrive. You’ll stabilize the injury, position the casualty into recovery position perhaps, rest them, reassure them.

Instructor: Don’t say, “I promise you everything’s going to be okay.” Never promise. You don’t know. But don’t say, “It looks bad. You might not make it.” Something in the middle: “Hey, you’re not alone, and I’m here to help.”

Instructor: Changes in the ABCs, update 911. And that might mean you have to update your Transport Decision. And then document.

6. Unresponsive & Responsive Casualties / Moving a Casualty

Video: Always start with a Scene Survey. Take charge of the situation, call out to attract bystanders to assist you. What happened? Are there still hazards to deal with? What is the mechanism of injury? Are there suspected injuries? Make the area safe for yourself and others. Put gloves on if you have them. Identify yourself and offer to help. Obtain consent and assess responsiveness.

Video: If the casualty is unresponsive, this is a life-threatening medical emergency. Call for medical aid. Once help is on the way, assess for life-threatening conditions by performing a Primary Survey.

Video: Airway: open the airway using the head-tilt/chin-lift and check the mouth for anything loose. Breathing: check for normal breathing for at least five seconds but no more than ten seconds. If the casualty is not breathing or has abnormal, irregular gasping breaths, start CPR right away.

Video: If the casualty is breathing normally, assess circulation. Take note of the skin condition and temperature. Perform a Rapid Body Survey to check for hidden injuries.

Video: If there are no injuries to manage, continue ongoing casualty care until handover to medical help. Ensure an open airway using the recovery position. Check breathing again, compare to the initial findings, reassess circulation, cover the casualty, manage shock, record findings, and give an oral report to medical aid.

Video: With the responsive casualty, the Scene Survey and Primary Survey can be much quicker. Identify yourself as a first aider and ask for help. Always get consent. Ask the responsive casualty what happened and what’s wrong with them.

Video: Airway and breathing are easily assessed if the casualty responds to questions. Perform a Rapid Body Survey to look for hidden injuries. Any change in level of consciousness, signs of shock, or if the casualty does not feel better quickly requires medical attention right away.

Instructor: If you find someone on their tummy, why do you want to roll them onto their back? You want to see any injuries. You want to make sure if there’s any breathing. You can’t really assess the breathing, you can’t check the ABCs if they’re on their tummy.

Video: A casualty who is found lying on their stomach may need to be moved for a Primary Survey. Begin the Primary Survey in the position found. Attempt to assess breathing for at least five seconds but no more than ten seconds. If normal breathing is not detected, position the arm closest to you above the casualty’s head, support the head and neck, and pull the casualty towards you by the hip. Once the casualty is on their back, continue with the Primary Survey.

Instructor: In practice: extend the arm closest to you over their head, tuck the far arm against the casualty’s side, cross the far foot over the near foot, grab and protect the head and neck, and roll them always towards you. If you have help, use it. If you can’t do it, don’t injure yourself. Remember, your safety comes first.

7. Shock & Fainting

Instructor: So why is shock important to recognize? Shock is when your body does not have enough oxygenated blood. When your body doesn’t have enough of what it wants, then you’ll see the signs and symptoms on the body: poor circulation, blue lips, blue nails.

Video: Shock is a condition in which the body does not get enough oxygenated blood. Any illness or injury will cause shock. Shock is progressive. It gets worse over time if unmanaged properly. As shock progresses, it leads to unconsciousness and even death.

Video: The casualty can have pale, grayish skin. It may be tinged at the lips, tongue, earlobes, and extremities. The skin will feel cool to the touch and clammy. Breathing gets faster and shallower, heart rate increases. The casualty may become weak, nauseous, confused, perhaps combative, and eventually lose consciousness.

Video: First aid for shock begins with first aid for the cause. Clear the airway, make it as easy as possible to breathe. Stop the bleeding or assist with the casualty’s medication. Cover the casualty to keep them warm. If conscious, position them on their back, or help them into a sitting position if it helps breathing. If unconscious, use the recovery position. Reassure them. Don’t give anything to eat or drink.

Instructor: Oxygenated blood to your sensitive body — it’s your brain, and when your brain doesn’t get enough of what it wants, it will shut down.

Video: Fainting is a loss of consciousness that lasts a very short time, no more than a few minutes. It is caused by a temporary shortage of oxygenated blood to the brain.

Video: Common reasons people faint are fear or anxiety, severe pain, injury or illness, long periods of standing or sitting, lack of fresh air, fatigue, or hunger.

Video: A person who feels faint often looks pale and is sweating. They may complain of nausea, dizziness, or feeling unsteady. Place the casualty flat on their back if possible, or at least sit them down. Ensure fresh air, loosen tight clothing at the neck, chest, and waist, and stay with them until fully recovered.

Video: If the casualty has fainted, they are unconscious, which means a breathing emergency because the tongue may fall back and block the airway, or fluids can pool at the back of the throat. Perform a Scene Survey and Primary Survey. If it’s just a faint, they should regain consciousness rapidly.

8. Heart Attack, Panic Attack, Stroke & CPR

Video: Out-of-hospital cardiac arrest caused by a heart attack is a leading cause of death. When a casualty experiences a heart attack, it is often caused by a narrowed artery in the heart, which does not allow adequate circulation to the heart muscle.

Video: If the narrowing is temporary, the pain or discomfort is called angina. It can be relieved with rest and medication, usually nitroglycerin. If the lack of circulation is profound, heart muscle can die due to a lack of oxygen.

Video: The casualty suffering angina or a heart attack may show signs of shock: pale or ashen skin, blue-tinged earlobes, lips, or fingertips, cold and clammy skin, rapid shallow breathing, and upper-body pain anywhere from the upper abdomen to the lower jaw and arms. The pain may be a broad discomfort or crushing, squeezing sensation rather than a sharp stabbing pain. They may feel nausea or vomit.

Video: As soon as you recognize the signs and symptoms of angina or heart attack, call or have a bystander call for medical help. Place the casualty at rest, usually semi-sitting, make them comfortable, reassure them, and help them take their prescribed medication if available.

Video: Nitroglycerin tablets or sprays are common medications for angina pain. Ask whether the casualty uses drugs to treat erectile dysfunction, such as Viagra. If they have taken these drugs, it is recommended they not take nitroglycerin because it may cause a significant drop in blood pressure.

Video: Spray under the tongue or place tablets under the tongue. They are not to be swallowed. Nitroglycerin may be repeated every 5 to 10 minutes if needed, to a maximum of three doses.

Instructor: One thing the video didn’t mention was if you are helping a casualty who is 19 years of age or younger who is experiencing a heart attack, we won’t administer aspirin. The reason why is because aspirin can increase the risk of Reye syndrome.

Instructor: We’re going to talk about anxiety and panic attack next. The reason why we talk about this right after a heart attack is because the signs and symptoms of an anxiety panic attack are so similar to that of a heart attack, which is also why we call 911 right away for this.

Instructor: While we wait for 911 to arrive, advise the casualty to regulate their breathing. We don’t use the brown paper bag method. You’re just inhaling the CO₂ that you’re exhaling into that paper bag, so it’s not fresh oxygen.

Instructor: Instead, use box breathing. Inhale through your nostrils for four seconds, hold the breath for four seconds, and exhale through your mouth for four seconds. It helps regulate that breathing so it’s not gasping.

Instructor: TIA stands for Transient Ischemic Attack. The signs and symptoms are the same as stroke, but with a TIA it’s not permanent. Damage that happens from a stroke is permanent, but a TIA is not.

Video: Remember FAST as a way to check for signs of a stroke and get immediate help. Facial droop: one side of the face doesn’t move as well as the other. Arm drift: have the casualty hold both arms out; one arm may not move or may drift down. Speech: the casualty slurs words, uses incorrect words, or is not able to speak. Time: get immediate medical help. Note the time the symptoms started.

Instructor: The biggest takeaway from FAST is time. When you recognize weakness on one side, call 911 right away.

Instructor: Whenever we’re putting our casualty into recovery position, we’re always rolling them towards us. However, if someone has a stroke and their left side is damaged, we don’t want to roll them onto their left side. We’re going to roll them onto their uninjured side, so the healthy side is on the ground and the damaged side is facing up.

Instructor: We’re going to get into CPR now. CPR stands for cardiopulmonary resuscitation. CPR is given to a casualty when they’re going through cardiac arrest, when they’re not breathing.

🔴 DEFINITELY ON EXAM: CPR ratio is 30 compressions followed by 2 breaths. Compressions come first, breaths after.

Video: Cardiac arrest needs immediate CPR until a defibrillator can be used to restore a normal heart rhythm. Perform a Scene Survey, obtain consent and assess responsiveness, call for medical aid and an AED, and perform a Primary Survey.

Video: If the casualty is not breathing or has abnormal, irregular gasping breaths, start CPR right away. Kneel facing the casualty so the hands can be placed mid-chest. Place the heel of one hand midline on the breastbone and the other hand on top.

Video: Compress the chest at least 5 centimetres / 2 inches at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute. Allow the chest to fully recoil. Give two ventilations with just enough force to make the chest rise. Take care not to over-ventilate.

Video: CPR is continued until an AED can be applied, the casualty begins breathing again, medical help or another rescuer takes over, or the first aider is too tired to continue.

9. Diabetes, Seizures, Asthma & Anaphylaxis

Video: In a diabetic emergency, the casualty may present rapidly with flushed, dry and warm skin, groggy drowsiness, and breath that often smells like nail polish remover.

Video: Perform a Scene Survey. Identify yourself and get consent. Ask what happened to learn the history. If the casualty can tell you what they need, help them take what is needed, usually sugar. If the casualty is confused, give them something sweet to eat or drink if there is no danger of choking.

Instructor: If they’re responsive, they’ll tell you what they need. A lot of the time diabetics will say, “I just need a sip of a juice box,” or “I have a granola bar in my purse, grab me that and give me a few bites.”

🔴 DEFINITELY ON EXAM: During an active seizure / convulsion, do not restrain or restrict the casualty’s movement.

Instructor: There’s an exam question that comes up with seizures. When someone’s having an active convulsion, they’re actively shaking, having a seizure, do we restrict their movement? No. We just let the seizure happen and we’ll help them afterwards.

Video: In a partial seizure, only part of the brain is affected. In a generalized seizure, the whole brain is affected. A casualty may experience an aura before a seizure.

Video: A typical generalized seizure has two phases. The tonic phase involves sudden loss of consciousness and rigidity. In the clonic phase, convulsions occur, sometimes violent convulsions.

Video: Do not try to restrain the casualty. Protect the airway, but never place anything in the casualty’s mouth. After the convulsions stop, the casualty may be confused and exhausted in the postictal state.

Instructor: Leave them. You don’t want to risk putting anything in their mouth to protect their teeth because there’s a choking risk. Our biggest concern is their head. If we can put something underneath their head, great. But if they’re shaking too much, just leave them and help them after.

Video: Asthma is a respiratory illness where the airways constrict, swell, and produce thick mucus. A severe asthma attack may cause coughing, wheezing, obvious respiratory distress, chest tightness, anxiety, and bluish lips or extremities.

Video: A casualty may adopt the tripod position, sitting upright and leaning forward, to make it easier to breathe. Help retrieve their metered-dose inhaler. Shake the inhaler, remove the cap, deliver the dose as they breathe in, and have them hold their breath for 10 seconds. A spacer is recommended when it is difficult to coordinate inhalation.

Instructor: Asthma, diabetes, anaphylaxis — get them to the hospital as soon as you can, so if they have a second episode it’s under the care of medical professionals.

Video: Allergic reactions, anaphylaxis, can quickly produce respiratory difficulty and low blood pressure. A casualty with severe allergies will have epinephrine available in an auto-injector.

Video: Signs can include trouble breathing, swelling of lips, tongue, and face, hives, nausea, vomiting, lightheadedness, and a feeling of doom.

🔴 EXAM CONNECTION: EpiPen / epinephrine auto-injector is used for severe allergic reaction / anaphylaxis.

Video: Press the orange or black end of the auto-injector firmly into the fleshy outside of the mid-thigh and hold for several seconds. Continue ongoing care and be prepared to assist with a second dose if symptoms reoccur.

10. Opioids, Hypothermia, Frostbite & Heat Injuries

Instructor: Opioids are a class of powerful drugs that are usually prescribed to treat severe pain. People may think of street opioids such as heroin and fentanyl.

Instructor: Opioid poisoning signs and symptoms can include blue, gray, or purple lips and nails, confusion, unconsciousness, pinpoint pupils, gulping, slobbering or snoring sounds, slow or no breathing, drowsiness, difficulty staying awake, and somnolence.

Instructor: Naloxone is the medication that helps counteract opioid poisoning or overdose. It can be administered nasally or by injection. It is not permanent, so once you administer naloxone, transport the casualty to hospital as soon as possible because the effect can wear off.

Instructor: SAVE ME is a step-by-step protocol that can help you know how to deal with an overdose. One thing I will say is your safety comes first with an opioid overdose and administering naloxone.

Instructor: “Why didn’t you administer naloxone?” “I didn’t feel safe. I found a broken pipe in their hand. They had a used needle in their pocket.” You’re allowed to say that. Your safety comes first. If you do not feel safe, document it, call 911. That’s it.

Instructor: The temperature of the body’s core is 37 degrees Celsius. If the body core temperature drops more than two degrees, the body’s tissues cannot function properly. This state of generalized cooling is called hypothermia.

Instructor: Hypothermia needs gradual rewarming, whereas frostbite needs rapid rewarming. The reason why we want to rewarm rapidly for frostbite is because we want to minimize the formation of ice crystals and introduce circulation back into those affected areas.

Instructor: Do not rewarm frostbite unless you are able to have a stable source of heat, because you don’t want to risk frostbite going cold again. Don’t thaw anything out until you have a stable source of hot water or heat that you can keep warm.

Instructor: Heat cramps are when your muscles have not much hydration. When your muscles don’t have water, they cramp up. Very painful.

Instructor: Heat exhaustion is intense labor in the heat. You’re working out in the sun, not getting enough shade breaks or water breaks.

Instructor: Heat stroke is dangerous, very. This is the most dangerous of the three, because heat stroke is when your body has gone too long without proper hydration, to the point where your body doesn’t even allow itself to generate sweat. Sweat is what helps you cool down.

Instructor: With frostbite and hypothermia, get them out of the cold into the heat. Same thing with heat injuries: out of the heat into the cold, reintroducing electrolytes and hydration back into the body.

11. Concussion & Occupational Stress Injury (OSI)

Instructor: If someone had a suspected concussion, could someone read them out for me, please?

Student: Loss of consciousness. Lying motionless on the ground, slow to get up, unsteady on feet, grabbing or clutching of head, dazed, blank or vacant look, confused, not aware of place or events.

Student: Signs and symptoms: headache, seizure or convulsion, confusion, nausea or vomiting, drowsiness, dizziness, difficulty concentrating, more emotional, irritability, sadness, fatigue or low energy, blurred vision, amnesia, sensitivity to light.

Instructor: Other than seeing the signs and symptoms or visible clues of a suspected concussion, another thing you can do is a memory test for your casualty.

14. Burns — Confirmed Exam Topic

Instructor: Burns and infection also need to be referred to medical aid. We’ll watch a video on burns and do a little bit of burn review because there’s some questions on the exam about it.

🔴 CONFIRMED EXAM TOPIC: Burns.

Video: Burns can be caused by heat, radiation, electricity, or chemicals. The skin protects the body from bacteria, helps regulate body temperature, and retains fluid. Damaged skin can’t perform these vital tasks.

Video: A burn is described as superficial, where the top layer is damaged, making it red and painful; partial thickness, where both layers of skin are damaged and blisters and swelling can happen; and full thickness, where the burn extends through the skin into the fat and muscle beneath.

Video: It’s not just the depth of a burn that makes it serious. The size of the burn and the location are critical as well.

Video: Heat burns, also called thermal burns, include burns from open flames, hot objects, hot liquid or steam, and friction. First aid involves cooling the burn right away with cool water or a clean wet cloth until the pain has lessened.

Video: Be careful cooling large-area burns because you may cause hypothermia. Loosen or remove anything tight such as jewelry or clothing before swelling. Don’t remove anything stuck to the burn. When pain has lessened, loosely cover with a clean lint-free dressing.

Video: Chemical burns can continue to burn as long as the chemical remains on the skin. Flush with large amounts of cool water. If the chemical is a dry powder, quickly brush off loose chemical before flushing. Continue flushing for 15 to 20 minutes.

Video: Electrical burns can have complications. Electricity can stop the heart, violently throw the casualty, and cause head or spinal injuries, fractures, or dislocations. Make sure there is no further danger from electricity. Never cut or move live wires.

Video: Look for entry and exit burns. Cover electrical burns with clean dry dressings. Give ongoing care, medical help, first aid for shock, and monitoring.

Video: Radiation burns include sunburn, X-rays, arc welder’s flash, and radiation from radioactive material. Get out of the sun, cool the area, and don’t break blisters because that can promote infection.

Instructor: You need to know the other terminology. Superficial burn is a first-degree burn. That’s when only the top layer of the skin is damaged.

🔴 DEFINITELY ON EXAM: Partial thickness / second-degree burn = blisters. If you see the word “blisters,” that is the clue.

Instructor: Partial-thickness burn, or second-degree burn, is when blisters begin to form. On the exam, when it describes a type of burn and you see the word blisters, that’s how you know it’s partial thickness. It’s now a second-degree burn. It’s no longer superficial.

Instructor: Third-degree burn, or full-thickness burn, is when multiple layers of the skin, maybe deep enough to see blood vessels, are damaged.

🔴 DEFINITELY ON EXAM: Hot coffee spilled on the leg = thermal burn.

Instructor: Another question on burns is going to come up: you spill hot coffee on your leg. What type of burn is that? Electrical, thermal, chemical? Thermal burn.
